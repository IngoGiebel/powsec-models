### Strategic Actor Analysis: Who Benefits from Bitcoin's Failure?

The preceding risk models assume exogenous shocks — price declines, regulatory shifts, or organic pollution growth. However, a game-theoretic lens reveals that multiple strategic actors have rational incentives to *actively precipitate* these shocks, transforming our risk vectors from passive vulnerabilities into potential attack surfaces.

**Nation-state actors and central banks.** Sovereign actors whose monetary policy sovereignty is threatened by Bitcoin adoption face a classic collective action problem. While no single central bank benefits from openly attacking Bitcoin (reputational risk, Streisand effect), coordinated regulatory action — synchronized exchange bans, mining prohibitions, or CBDC mandates — represents a dominant strategy when Bitcoin's market capitalization threatens fiat monetary transmission mechanisms. China's 2021 mining ban demonstrated that unilateral action merely redistributes hashrate; effective suppression requires multilateral coordination, which game theory predicts is unstable absent a credible commitment mechanism. However, the mere *threat* of coordinated action functions as a Damocles sword over institutional adoption decisions.

**Short sellers and derivatives traders.** The maturation of Bitcoin derivatives markets (CME futures, options, perpetual swaps) has created a class of actors who directly profit from price declines. A well-capitalized short seller faces an asymmetric payoff: triggering a cascade costs finite capital (e.g., a large spot market sell-off), while the resulting leveraged liquidation cascade on derivatives exchanges amplifies returns multiplicatively. Our institutional exit model (Section 3.4) quantifies the *downstream* impact, but the *upstream* trigger could be a deliberate, strategically timed sell-off designed to breach key technical support levels and trigger algorithmic liquidations. The game-theoretic incentive is clear: with approximately $30B in daily spot volume, a coordinated sell-off of $2–5B could trigger cascading liquidations worth multiples of the initial position — a rational strategy given sufficient capital and risk appetite.

**Competing blockchain ecosystems.** Ethereum's transition to Proof-of-Stake (PoS) created an implicit competitive dynamic: every dollar of institutional allocation to Bitcoin is a dollar not allocated to PoS chains. While overt attacks would damage crypto credibility broadly, subtle narrative warfare — amplifying Bitcoin's energy consumption criticism, funding content pollution research, or lobbying for PoW-specific regulation (as attempted in the EU's MiCA deliberations) — represents a low-cost, plausibly-deniable strategy. The game-theoretic equilibrium depends on whether investors view BTC and ETH as complements or substitutes.

**Incumbent financial institutions.** Traditional financial intermediaries face disintermediation risk from Bitcoin adoption. Banks earning revenue from cross-border transfers, custodial services, and monetary intermediation have a rational interest in regulatory capture — supporting compliance frameworks so onerous that only licensed financial institutions can offer Bitcoin exposure, effectively re-intermediating the disintermediation. The irony of spot Bitcoin ETFs is that they simultaneously legitimize Bitcoin *and* channel its adoption through incumbent gatekeepers, creating a Nash equilibrium where banks profit from Bitcoin's success while retaining the option to advocate for restrictive regulation if self-custody adoption threatens their business model.

**Large miners as strategic consolidators.** Our miner capitulation model (Section 3.1) assumes miners are price-takers. However, the largest mining operations (Marathon Digital, Riot Platforms) hold significant BTC treasury positions and could theoretically benefit from short-term price suppression that eliminates smaller competitors, followed by post-capitulation difficulty reduction and price recovery. This predatory pricing strategy — absorbing short-term losses to acquire market share at reduced difficulty — is rational when (a) the firm has sufficient balance sheet to survive the downturn, (b) eliminated competitors' hashrate permanently exits, and (c) the expected post-consolidation revenue exceeds the cost of the suppression period. The Nakamoto Coefficient of 3 identified in our analysis makes this strategy especially concerning.

**Intelligence agencies and surveillance states.** Bitcoin's pseudonymous transparency creates a surveillance paradox: the blockchain is simultaneously private (pseudonymous addresses) and maximally transparent (every transaction permanently visible). Intelligence agencies may rationally prefer Bitcoin to remain viable but not dominant — large enough to serve as a traceable financial surveillance tool, small enough not to threaten monetary sovereignty. This creates a game-theoretic equilibrium where certain state actors simultaneously *regulate* Bitcoin publicly while *utilizing* its surveillance properties covertly, a dual-use dynamic with no stable endpoint.

**Implications for risk assessment.** The existence of multiple actors with rational incentives to attack Bitcoin transforms our four risk vectors from independent stochastic processes into *correlated* threats with potential strategic triggers. A game-theoretic synthesis suggests that the most dangerous scenario is not any single risk vector in isolation, but a coordinated or coincidental alignment where (i) a derivatives-funded short attack triggers price decline, (ii) activating miner capitulation that increases concentration, (iii) while regulatory pressure amplifies institutional exit incentives. The probability of such alignment increases as more actors recognize and prepare for it — a self-reinforcing dynamic that traditional financial risk models, calibrated to uncorrelated risk factors, systematically underestimate.
